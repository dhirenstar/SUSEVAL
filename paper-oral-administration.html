SUSEVAL: The Effect of Administration Modality on Usability Perceptions Measured Using the System Usability Scale

Dhiren Chetty · Supervisor: Melissa Densmore · Download Paper (PDF) ↓

This study examined whether reading the "Standard" English System Usability Scale aloud, rather than presenting it in written form, systematically alters SUS scores or affects how participants interpret questionnaire items. It forms the oral administration arm of the SUSEVAL project.

Research questions

  • RQ1: How does the mode of administration (oral versus written) relate to SUS scores and internal consistency for the "Standard" English SUS?
  • RQ2: How do participants experience the presence of an interviewer during oral SUS administration in terms of comprehension, engagement, and responding, and how do these experiences help interpret the findings in RQ1?

Background

Finstad identified that non-native English speakers frequently encounter comprehension difficulties with the SUS vocabulary, notably the word "cumbersome" in Item 8. Broader survey methodology identifies established mode effects: interviewer-administered questionnaires can improve attention and allow respondents to seek clarification, but may also introduce social-desirability bias. Evidence from other psychometric instruments has shown no consistent directional effect of reading questions aloud.

Methodology

Experimental Modality Procedure (N = 25, 150 Evaluations)
Phase 1 Written Administration

Participants completed the "Standard" English SUS through Microsoft Forms, rating three everyday systems.

Phase 2 Live Oral Administration

The researcher read each SUS item aloud during WhatsApp calls, and participants responded using verbal Likert categories. Clarification requests were recorded.

Post-session Participant Reflections

Participants reflected on their experiences of the two administration modes, including wording difficulties and perceived differences between them.

Analysis Mixed-Methods Analysis

Quantitative analyses included a paired-samples t-test, linear mixed-effects modelling, and Cronbach's α. Qualitative responses were examined using reflexive thematic analysis.

Study design and participants

  • Experimental Design: Within-subjects, mixed-methods comparative design. Each participant rated three everyday systems in writing (via Microsoft Forms) and three different systems orally (via a synchronous WhatsApp call where the researcher read each item aloud and transcribed the response).
  • Presentation Order: All participants completed the written condition before the oral condition. The order was not counterbalanced, so order effects cannot be separated from administration-mode effects.
  • Pilot Pretesting: Conducted with three participants. A WhatsApp voice-note alternative was evaluated for the oral condition but was discarded due to tediousness; response options were changed to verbal categories (Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree) rather than numerical points (1 to 5).
  • Participant Cohort: 25 individuals (17 University of Cape Town students, 5 students at other tertiary institutions, 2 unemployed adults, 1 employed professional), generating 150 evaluations. Primary home languages: English (23), isiZulu (1), Shona (1).

  • Paired Mean Comparison: The observed mean difference (oral minus written) was −1.93 points (95% CI −6.61 to 2.75, t = −0.853, p = .402, Cohen's dz = −0.17). A Wilcoxon signed-rank test also found no statistically significant shift (p = .184). These results indicate that this study did not detect a statistically significant difference; they do not establish that the two modes are equivalent.
  • Linear Mixed-Effects Modelling: In a regression model with participant as random intercept and system as fixed effect, oral modality was associated with a 2.49-point lower score (p = .302). System type had a strong, statistically significant effect (p < .001); Google Search achieved the highest usability ratings and EskomSePush the lowest.

Participant qualitative experiences

“Oral, the interviewer could clarify and could ask more questions if I felt like I needed to.”

  • Modality Affordances: Written administration allowed participants to proceed at their own pace and re-read items (n = 16); oral administration allowed participants to request clarification in real time (n = 12).
  • Item Wording Friction: Most participants found the questionnaire generally understandable (n = 19). However, 10 participants explicitly flagged the word "cumbersome", including participants described in the study records as native or fluent English speakers.
  • Interviewer Dynamics: Five participants reported the interviewer made the evaluation feel conversational and engaging; conversely, four noted heightened pressure, perceived scrutiny, or social desirability concerns.
  • Perceived Equivalence: Twenty-one participants felt both modes captured identical perceptions. Four participants were reported to have said that verbal clarification affected the answer they would otherwise have selected.

Discussion and implications

This study did not detect a statistically significant difference in SUS scores between written and oral administration. Cronbach's alpha indicated high internal consistency in both conditions, although these estimates do not establish that one mode is inherently more reliable than the other. Participant reflections highlighted different experiences: written administration supported independent pacing and rereading, while oral administration enabled immediate clarification but could also introduce interviewer-related pressure. The word "cumbersome" was a reported difficulty for some participants, including some fluent English speakers; the findings do not establish that vocabulary difficulty is independent of administration mode or language proficiency.

Limitations and conclusion

Methodological Limitations: The participant sample comprised 25 individuals, predominantly university students with high English fluency; proficiency was self-reported; the administration order was strictly written-first; and live interviewer presence introduces potential observer effects. The planned investigation into low-proficiency English speakers could not be addressed in this sample.

Overall, the results suggest that oral and written administration produced similar SUS scores in this sample, with no statistically significant difference detected. This should not be interpreted as proof of equivalence, particularly given the small sample and the fixed written-first order. Future research should include more diverse participants and counterbalance administration order to help distinguish mode effects from order effects.
