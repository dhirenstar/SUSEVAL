SUSEVAL: The Effect of Administration Modality on Usability Perceptions Measured Using the System Usability Scale

Dhiren Chetty · Full paper (PDF)

This paper asked whether reading the standard English SUS aloud, instead of showing it in writing, changes scores or how participants engage with it. It is the oral arm of SUSEVAL.

Research questions

  • RQ1: How does administration mode (oral versus written) relate to SUS scores and internal consistency?
  • RQ2: How do participants experience an interviewer during oral administration, and how does this help interpret RQ1?

Background

Finstad found that non-native English speakers struggle with wording such as "cumbersome". Survey research reports mode effects: interviewers can help comprehension and focus, but may add social-desirability bias. Evidence from other instruments shows no consistent effect of reading questions aloud. The author knew of no SUS translated or validated in any South African language.

Methodology

  • Design: within-subjects, mixed methods. Each participant rated three systems in writing (Microsoft Forms) and three orally (a WhatsApp call in which the researcher read each item aloud and recorded the answer).
  • Order: written always came first, so order and mode were confounded.
  • Pretest: three participants. The voice-note option was dropped, and answers were given as verbal categories (Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree) rather than numbers.
  • Participants: 25 people (17 UCT students, five students elsewhere, two unemployed, one employed), giving 150 evaluations. Home languages: English (23), isiZulu (1), Shona (1).
  • Analysis: paired t-test and Wilcoxon test at participant level, a linear mixed-effects model with system as a fixed effect and participant as a random intercept, Cronbach's alpha, and reflexive thematic analysis.

Results

  • The mean difference (oral minus written) was −1.93 points (95% CI −6.61 to 2.75), t = −0.853, p = .402, dz = −0.17. The Wilcoxon test gave p = .184.
  • In the mixed model, oral administration was associated with a 2.49-point lower score (p = .302). System had a significant effect (p < .001); Google Search scored highest and EskomSePush lowest.

Participant accounts (counts as reported)

  • Affordances: written gave control to reread and take time (n = 16); oral allowed clarification (n = 12).
  • Wording: most found the questionnaire understandable (n = 19); "cumbersome" was flagged by ten, including fluent or native English speakers.
  • Interviewer influence: some felt it was more human and conversational (n = 5); some felt pressure or judgement (n = 4).
  • Equivalence: most saw the modes as asking the same thing (n = 21); four said clarification changed an answer.

Discussion and implications

Scores were similar and reliability was high in both modes, yet experience differed. Oral administration may be a useful alternative where participants benefit from hearing items, although it can influence answers. Some difficulty came from the SUS wording itself, not from mode or proficiency.

Limitations and conclusion

Limitations. Twenty-five participants, mostly fluent or native English speakers and mostly students; self-reported proficiency; fixed written-first order; unequal system allocation after non-response; and the interviewer's presence. The planned question about low-proficiency English speakers could not be addressed.

The paper concludes that oral and written administration produced broadly comparable scores while differing in engagement, and calls for larger, more linguistically diverse samples.
