SUSEVAL: The Effect of Administration Modality on Usability Perceptions Measured Using the System Usability Scale

Dhiren Chetty · Supervisor: Melissa Densmore · Download Paper (PDF) ↓

This paper investigated whether reading the standard English System Usability Scale aloud, rather than presenting it visually in writing, systematically alters scores or affects how participants interpret questionnaire items. It constitutes the oral administration arm of the SUSEVAL project.

Research questions

  • RQ1: How does administration mode (oral versus written) relate to SUS scores and psychometric internal consistency?
  • RQ2: How do participants experience an interviewer during oral administration, and how does this contextualize the quantitative findings of RQ1?

Background

Prior psychometric research by Finstad demonstrated that non-native English speakers frequently encounter comprehension obstacles with idiosyncratic SUS vocabulary, notably the word "cumbersome" in Item 8. Broader survey methodology identifies established mode effects: live interviewers can improve attention and resolve ambiguities, but may simultaneously introduce social-desirability bias. Evidence from other psychometric instruments has shown no consistent directional effect of reading questions aloud.

Methodology

Experimental Modality Procedure (N = 25, 150 Evaluations)
Phase 1 Written Administration

Microsoft Forms: participants self-administer standard English SUS across 3 everyday systems.

Phase 2 Live Oral Session

WhatsApp audio call: researcher reads each item aloud; participant responds using verbal Likert anchors.

Phase 3 Clarification Logging

Spontaneous participant queries regarding item phrasing (e.g., "cumbersome") logged and documented.

Phase 4 Mixed Analysis

Paired t-tests, mixed-effects regression, Cronbach's α, and reflexive thematic analysis.

  • Experimental Design: Within-subjects, mixed-methods comparative design. Each participant rated three everyday systems in writing (via Microsoft Forms) and three different systems orally (via a synchronous WhatsApp voice call where the researcher read each item aloud and logged the response).
  • Presentation Order: The written condition consistently preceded the oral condition; consequently, presentation order and administration mode were deliberately linked in this protocol.
  • Pilot Pretesting: Conducted with three participants. An asynchronous voice-note alternative was evaluated and discarded due to high friction; response options were standardized to verbal categories (Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree) rather than raw numerical points.
  • Participant Cohort: 25 individuals (17 University of Cape Town students, 5 students at other tertiary institutions, 2 unemployed adults, 1 employed professional), generating 150 evaluations. Primary home languages: English (23), isiZulu (1), Shona (1).

Results

  • Paired Mean Comparison: The observed mean difference (oral minus written) was −1.93 points (95% CI −6.61 to 2.75, t = −0.853, p = .402, Cohen's dz = −0.17). A non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test confirmed no significant shift (p = .184).
  • Linear Mixed-Effects Modelling: In a regression model with participant as random intercept and system as fixed effect, oral modality was associated with a 2.49-point lower score (p = .302). System type exerted a strong, statistically significant effect (p < .001); Google Search achieved the highest usability ratings and EskomSePush the lowest.

Participant qualitative experiences

“When doing it orally, I could pause and ask what cumbersome meant in that context. In the written form, I just had to guess.”

  • Modality Affordances: Written administration afforded personal pacing and the autonomy to re-read items (cited by n = 16); oral administration afforded real-time clarification for ambiguous terms (cited by n = 12).
  • Item Wording Friction: The vast majority found the overall questionnaire understandable (n = 19); however, the word "cumbersome" was explicitly flagged by 10 participants, including native and fluent English speakers.
  • Interviewer Dynamics: Five participants reported the interviewer made the evaluation feel conversational and engaging; conversely, four noted heightened pressure, perceived scrutiny, or social desirability concerns.
  • Perceived Equivalence: Twenty-one participants felt both modes captured identical perceptions; four stated that verbal clarification directly altered the answer they would otherwise have selected.

Discussion and implications

Usability scores remained statistically comparable and psychometric reliability was exceptionally high across both modalities; however, qualitative engagement differed substantially. Oral administration provides a viable alternative for usability testing where participants benefit from hearing questions, although researchers must remain cognizant of interviewer influence. Notably, comprehension stumbling blocks stemmed from archaic SUS vocabulary itself rather than modality or general linguistic capability.

Limitations and conclusion

Methodological Limitations: The participant sample comprised 25 individuals, predominantly university students with high English fluency; proficiency was self-reported; the administration order was strictly written-first; and live interviewer presence introduces potential observer effects. The planned investigation into low-proficiency English speakers could not be addressed in this sample.

The paper concludes that oral and written administration produced broadly comparable scores while engaging participants differently. The author calls for future investigations among broader demographic cohorts.
