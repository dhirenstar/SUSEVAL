Evaluating the System Usability Scale for South African languages

Usability questionnaires are written in English, yet many South Africans use English as an additional language. SUSEVAL asked what happens to the System Usability Scale (SUS) when it is translated into isiXhosa and Afrikaans, or read aloud instead of read on screen.

Background and motivation

The SUS is a ten-item questionnaire that turns a person's agreement with ten statements into a perceived-usability score from 0 to 100. It was written in English. South Africa has twelve official languages, and English is the home language of a minority of people in the Western Cape, although it dominates universities and workplaces.

The team found little published work on the SUS in South African languages or on how wording and administration shape responses. In earlier research the word "cumbersome" in item 8 was already known to trouble non-native English speakers.

Project aims

  • Adapt the SUS into isiXhosa and Afrikaans through a documented process.
  • Compare scores and participant experience between language versions.
  • Compare oral with written administration of the English SUS.

Project overview

Three students each ran one arm of a shared study at the University of Cape Town. Participants were adults recruited through university channels, community networks and snowball sampling. They were contacted through WhatsApp and completed written forms in Microsoft Forms.

  • Each participant rated six everyday systems: ATMs, EskomSePush, Google Search, point-of-sale machines, Takealot and Uber.
  • Every participant completed three ratings in each of two conditions, always written English first and a second condition after it, with different systems in each.
  • Each arm added reflexive thematic analysis of participant feedback.
Scope. Samples were small and purposive. The project did not validate any instrument, and it did not test measurement equivalence.

Key findings

In all three arms, mean SUS scores in the two conditions were close, and no difference was statistically detectable. Bars show mean scores out of 100.

  • Internal consistency was good in the two arms that reported it: Cronbach's alpha was .843 (English) and .890 (isiXhosa), and .878 (written) and .931 (oral).
  • Participants often found English easier because of familiarity with written English and technology terms, but ease and preference did not always match.
  • Oral administration let participants ask for clarification, and it also created pressure for some.
  • Afrikaans interviewees understood the adapted wording but consistently found English easier; particular terms, such as inkonsekwenthede, caused difficulty.
Qualifications. Written English always came first, and each participant rated different systems in each condition. Similar means therefore do not show that the versions are equivalent, and the designs cannot isolate a language effect. Findings apply mainly to fluent English speakers.

The three research papers

Inhle Cele

When the Questionnaire Speaks isiXhosa but the System Speaks English

Developed an isiXhosa SUS and compared it with English among 28 isiXhosa home-language speakers. Contribution: a documented adaptation process and evidence that written-language familiarity and register shape the experience.

Read paper details →

Dhiren Chetty

The Effect of Administration Modality on Usability Perceptions

Compared oral and written administration of the English SUS among 25 participants. Contribution: evidence that scores were broadly comparable while engagement differed.

Read paper details →

Yazeed Adams

Cross-cultural adaptation of the System Usability Scale into Afrikaans

Adapted the SUS into Afrikaans (AF-SUS) and explored how seven bilingual participants experienced it. Contribution: a documented adaptation, with the final item wording published.
