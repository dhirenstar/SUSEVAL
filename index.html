Evaluating the System Usability Scale for South African languages

Usability questionnaires are routinely administered in English, yet many South Africans speak English as an additional language. SUSEVAL examined what happens to the System Usability Scale (SUS) when it is translated into isiXhosa and Afrikaans, or read aloud instead of completed on screen.

Background and motivation

The System Usability Scale (SUS) is an established ten-item psychometric questionnaire that converts a respondent's agreement with ten standardized statements into a perceived-usability composite score from 0 to 100. It was originally authored in English. South Africa recognizes twelve official languages, and English is the primary home language of only a minority of people in the Western Cape, despite its pervasive dominance across university education, technology interfaces, and professional workplaces.

The research team identified a critical absence of published investigations on the SUS in indigenous South African languages or on how wording complexity and administration modality shape user responses. In prior literature, specific vocabulary such as the word "cumbersome" in Item 8 has consistently been demonstrated to introduce cognitive friction and miscomprehension among non-native English speakers.

Project aims

  • Cross-Cultural Adaptation: Adapt the SUS into isiXhosa and Afrikaans through rigorous, documented forward- and back-translation processes.
  • Score & Experience Comparison: Quantitatively compare usability scores and qualitatively examine participant experiences between language versions.
  • Modality Evaluation: Compare oral administration with written administration of the English SUS to understand accessibility and interviewer effects.

Project overview

Three honours students each conducted an independent arm of a unified research project at the University of Cape Town. Participants were adults recruited through university student networks, community channels, and snowball sampling. Participant interactions and recruitment were coordinated through WhatsApp, and participants completed written questionnaires via Microsoft Forms.

Condition 1: Written English Baseline

Every participant evaluated three systems using the standard written English SUS.

Condition 2: Experimental Arm

Participants evaluated three distinct systems under the experimental condition: isiXhosa written, Oral English, or Afrikaans written.

  • Six Everyday Systems Evaluated: ATMs, EskomSePush, Google Search, point-of-sale (POS) card terminals, Takealot, and Uber.
  • Within-Subjects Design: Every participant completed three ratings in each of two conditions, always written English first and the second condition after it, with different systems evaluated across conditions.
  • Qualitative Investigation: Each arm incorporated reflexive thematic analysis of participant feedback, open-ended commentary, and post-task interviews.
Scope and Qualifications: Participant samples were purposive and modest in size. The project was exploratory; it did not validate any psychometric instrument, nor did it establish formal measurement equivalence or invariance across populations.

Qualitative Insights & Thematic Findings

  • High Internal Consistency: Reliability remained robust across arms reporting Cronbach's alpha: .843 (English) and .890 (isiXhosa) in Cele's arm; .878 (written) and .931 (oral) in Chetty's arm.
  • Familiarity vs. Preference: Participants consistently reported English was easier to read due to habitual exposure to English user interfaces and technical vocabulary; however, ease did not always correlate with subjective language preference or cultural resonance.
  • Modality Trade-offs: Oral administration afforded opportunities for real-time clarification and conversational warmth, but simultaneously introduced social-desirability pressures or perceived judgment for some respondents.
  • Linguistic Register and Vocabulary Friction: In the Afrikaans arm, bilingual participants understood the translated statements but found English easier; hyper-formal terminology such as inkonsekwenthede created cognitive stumbling blocks.
Methodological Qualifications: Written English was consistently administered first, and participants evaluated different technologies in each condition. Similar group means do not demonstrate that translated versions are psychometrically equivalent, and the study designs could not completely isolate language effects from system and order effects. Findings generalize primarily to bilingual speakers with strong English proficiency. See Future Work for recommended research directions to address these constraints.

Future work

While SUSEVAL provides the first documented baseline for translated and oral SUS administration in South Africa, these exploratory findings highlight key methodological and design priorities for subsequent research.

Empirical Validation

Wider study to validate instruments

The current investigations were exploratory, conducted with modest participant cohorts (N = 28, 25, and 7) composed largely of university students with high baseline English fluency. A wider, large-scale empirical study is essential to formally validate the psychometric properties of the translated instruments:

  • Broader, Diverse Populations: Evaluate the instruments across wider segments of the South African population, specifically sampling across diverse educational backgrounds, age brackets, geographic areas, and degrees of English literacy.
  • Formal Psychometric Invariance: Conduct confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and measurement invariance testing to ensure that the adapted items measure the same underlying usability construct across different language groups without systematic measurement bias.
  • Fully Counterbalanced Designs: Counterbalance language presentation order and evaluate identical software systems across conditions to isolate language effects cleanly from presentation order and system-level difficulty.
Instrument Design

Multilingual & dynamic instruments

Rather than constraining bilingual respondents to a single monolingual instrument (forcing an either/or choice between English and a translated version), future usability evaluations should adopt a flexible, multilingual instrument architecture:

  • Multiple Languages Available: Make multiple official languages readily available simultaneously within the evaluation interface, allowing participants to choose their preferred language at the outset.
  • Freely Switch Between Languages: Allow participants to dynamically switch between languages or toggle parallel bilingual phrasing (e.g. isiXhosa/English or Afrikaans/English) item-by-item as they complete the questionnaire.
  • Accommodating Natural Code-Switching: Multilingual South Africans frequently navigate digital interfaces in English while speaking indigenous languages at home. A multilingual instrument honors cultural identity and comfort while preventing cognitive friction from unfamiliar formal terms (such as inkonsekwenthede or nkqubo) by providing immediate English reference where helpful.
