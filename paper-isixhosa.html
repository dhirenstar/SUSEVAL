When the Questionnaire Speaks isiXhosa but the System Speaks English: Adapting the System Usability Scale in South Africa

Inhle Cele · Full paper (PDF)

This paper developed an isiXhosa SUS and compared it with the English version among 28 isiXhosa first-language speakers. It is the isiXhosa arm of SUSEVAL.

Research questions

  • RQ1: How do SUS scores differ between the written English and written isiXhosa conditions?
  • RQ2: How do those differences vary with the language participants felt most comfortable completing the study in? This question was exploratory, because the grouping variable replaced a planned English-proficiency comparison after the demographics were inspected.

Background

Translating a questionnaire means preserving semantic, idiomatic, experiential and conceptual meaning, not only words. The paper followed Beaton et al. and Ortiz-Gutiérrez and Cruz-Avelar. High internal consistency does not by itself show that two language versions are equivalent. The authors found no earlier isiXhosa SUS.

Methodology

Adaptation

  1. Two forward translations: one by the researcher with Google Translate, one by a fluent isiXhosa-speaking engineer.
  2. Synthesis into one version by an isiXhosa language specialist.
  3. Back-translation by two independent bilingual speakers.
  4. Discrepancy review of items 3, 5, 6 and 9 (item 2 also noted). One back-translation reversed the polarity of item 3, which the specialist judged an error of the back-translation. No wording changed.
  5. Pretesting with two isiXhosa speakers.

Study design

Each of 28 participants rated three systems with the written English SUS and then three other systems with the written isiXhosa SUS. English always came first. The sample was 57.1% aged 18–24 and 67.9% women. Most (89.3%) reported isiXhosa as their only home language, and 50.0% said English was their most comfortable language, 46.4% isiXhosa and one participant Afrikaans.

Analysis used 168 evaluations (three per condition per participant). The primary model was an evaluation-level regression with participant and system fixed effects. Secondary checks were an unadjusted paired comparison, three re-specifications and a small same-system check. Fourteen participants gave qualitative feedback, analysed with reflexive thematic analysis.

Results

RQ2 language-by-comfort interaction 2.43 −8.21 to 13.07 .643
  • Mean SUS was 74.85 (SD 9.81) in English and 75.48 (SD 11.56) in isiXhosa.
  • Cronbach's alpha was .843 for English and .890 for isiXhosa, calculated over 84 clustered evaluations per language and so reported descriptively.
  • Differences between individuals were wide (SD about 13.5), and the largest changes may partly reflect different systems.
  • An exploratory comparison by education level was hypothesis-generating only.

Participant accounts

  • Familiarity made English easier. Participants cited exposure to written English, technology terms and English surveys, not English being clearer.
  • Understanding and naturalness differed. Some found the isiXhosa wording formal or translation-like yet understandable; some reported unfamiliar words affecting whether the versions seemed equivalent. The English word "cumbersome" was also unfamiliar to some.
  • Ease and preference did not always align. One participant preferred English for speed and isiXhosa for comfort.

Discussion and implications

Home language, easiest written language and preferred language were not always the same. Register and familiarity with formal written isiXhosa mattered alongside first-language identity. Translating the questionnaire does not make the evaluated technology available in isiXhosa. Future studies could test a bilingual or orally administered isiXhosa SUS; neither was evaluated here.

Limitations and conclusion

Limitations. Written English always preceded isiXhosa, and the systems differed between conditions, so language, order and system cannot be separated. The sample was small, purposive and mostly highly proficient in English. Contributors to the adaptation were a mix of professional and non-professional, and the pretest had two participants. The study does not establish measurement invariance or full validity.

No statistically detectable average difference emerged between written English and isiXhosa. The paper recommends a counterbalanced design, supervised sessions and larger samples.
