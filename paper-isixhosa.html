When the Questionnaire Speaks isiXhosa but the System Speaks English: Adapting the System Usability Scale in South Africa

Inhle Cele · Supervisor: Melissa Densmore · Download Paper (PDF) ↓

This paper developed an isiXhosa adaptation of the System Usability Scale and compared it with the standard English version among 28 isiXhosa first-language speakers. It constitutes the isiXhosa arm of the SUSEVAL project at the University of Cape Town.

Research questions

  • RQ1: How do SUS scores differ between the written English and written isiXhosa conditions?
  • RQ2: How do those differences vary with the language participants felt most comfortable completing the study in? (This question was exploratory, because the grouping variable replaced a planned English-proficiency comparison after participant demographics were inspected.)

Background

Translating a psychometric questionnaire requires preserving semantic, idiomatic, experiential, and conceptual equivalence rather than performing literal word-for-word substitution. This study followed established cross-cultural guidelines from Beaton et al. and Ortiz-Gutiérrez and Cruz-Avelar. High internal consistency alone does not demonstrate that two language versions measure the same construct identically. Prior to this research, no published adaptation of the SUS in isiXhosa existed.

Methodology

Adaptation workflow

Documented Adaptation Process (Beaton et al. Framework)
Phase 1 Forward Translation

Two independent translations: researcher with Google Translate, and a fluent bilingual engineer.

Phase 2 Expert Synthesis

An isiXhosa language specialist synthesized translations into a unified consensus version.

Phase 3 Back-Translation

Two independent bilingual speakers blind back-translated items to English.

Phase 4 Discrepancy Review

Specialist resolved polarity inversion on item 3 as back-translation error; confirmed items 2, 5, 6, 9.

Phase 5 Pretesting

Cognitive debriefing with 2 isiXhosa speakers to confirm semantic clarity.

Study design and procedure

Each of 28 participants rated three systems using the written English SUS and then three different systems using the written isiXhosa SUS. Written English always preceded isiXhosa. The participant sample was 57.1% aged 18–24 and 67.9% women. The vast majority (89.3%) reported isiXhosa as their sole home language; 50.0% reported English as their most comfortable language for study completion, 46.4% isiXhosa, and one participant Afrikaans.

Quantitative analysis was conducted on 168 distinct evaluations (three per condition per participant). The primary analytical specification was an evaluation-level regression incorporating participant and system fixed effects. Secondary sensitivity analyses included an unadjusted paired comparison, three alternative re-specifications, and a small same-system validation check. Fourteen participants provided detailed qualitative feedback, analysed through reflexive thematic analysis.

  • Descriptive Means: Mean SUS score was 74.85 (SD 9.81) in the English condition and 75.48 (SD 11.56) in the isiXhosa condition.
  • Internal Consistency: Cronbach's alpha was .843 for English and .890 for isiXhosa (calculated over 84 clustered evaluations per language and reported descriptively).
  • Individual Variation: Variation between individuals was substantial (standard deviation approximately 13.5), with larger differences partly driven by differing systems evaluated across conditions.
  • Education Comparison: An exploratory comparison by educational attainment was hypothesis-generating only.

Participant qualitative accounts

“I am so used to seeing tech words in English that seeing formal isiXhosa made me stop and think, even though it is my home language.”

  • Familiarity made English easier: Participants cited daily exposure to written English, technology interfaces, and online surveys rather than English possessing inherent clarity.
  • Understanding and naturalness differed: Several participants found the isiXhosa translation formal or textbook-like yet completely understandable; unfamiliar vocabulary terms affected subjective perceptions of equivalence. The English term "cumbersome" was similarly unfamiliar to several bilingual respondents.
  • Ease and preference did not always align: Participants drew a distinction between cognitive convenience (preferring English for speed) and cultural comfort or personal identity (preferring isiXhosa for dignity and representation).

Final isiXhosa SUS translation

Following the documented five-phase adaptation process—comprising independent forward translations, synthesis by an isiXhosa language specialist, blind back-translation, discrepancy resolution, and cognitive pretesting—the finalized ten-item isiXhosa System Usability Scale instrument is presented below. Items are scored on a standard 5-point Likert scale (1 = Andivumelani kwaphela / Strongly Disagree to 5 = Ndivumelana ngamandla / Strongly Agree).

Discussion and implications

Home language, easiest written language, and preferred language were not synonymous in this multilingual South African context. Linguistic register and habitual familiarity with formal written isiXhosa mattered profoundly alongside first-language cultural identity. Importantly, translating a questionnaire into an indigenous language does not make the underlying software system available in that language when the interfaces themselves remain exclusively in English. Future work could investigate bilingual questionnaire formats or orally administered isiXhosa surveys.

Limitations and conclusion

Methodological Limitations: Written English consistently preceded isiXhosa, and evaluated systems differed between conditions; consequently, language, presentation order, and system characteristics cannot be fully disentangled. The sample was modest in size, purposively recruited, and composed largely of individuals with high English fluency. The pretesting phase included two participants. This study does not establish measurement invariance or comprehensive psychometric validation.

In conclusion, no statistically detectable difference emerged between written English and isiXhosa SUS scores. The paper recommends that future investigations employ counterbalanced designs, supervised administration sessions, and larger, linguistically diverse sample cohorts.
