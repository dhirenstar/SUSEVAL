Cross-cultural adaptation of the System Usability Scale into Afrikaans

Yazeed Adams · Full paper (PDF)

This paper presented AF-SUS, an Afrikaans adaptation of the SUS, and examined it with Afrikaans-speaking participants who completed both versions. It is the Afrikaans arm of SUSEVAL.

Research questions

  • RQ1: How can the SUS be cross-culturally adapted into Afrikaans?
  • RQ2: How do Afrikaans-speaking participants experience the English and Afrikaans versions?
  • RQ3: What exploratory differences in SUS scores appeared between the versions?

Background

Questionnaires do not transfer through word-for-word translation. The study followed Ortiz-Gutiérrez and Cruz-Avelar's simplification of Beaton et al.'s guidelines and used Pan and Fond's coding scheme to classify translation errors. Afrikaans has varied registers, so the adaptation aimed for neutral, widely understood wording.

Methodology

  1. Two forward translations, one by the researcher and one by Google Translate.
  2. Synthesis by an Afrikaans language expert, who coded the errors.
  3. Pretesting with six participants; two of them also produced back-translations. Participants stood in for professional back-translators because of resource limits, so these served as evidence of interpretation, not formal validation.
  4. Final revision by the expert.

The main study used a within-subjects design. Seven participants each rated three systems in English and three different systems in Afrikaans; 39 of 42 expected evaluations were completed. Three took part in semi-structured interviews, analysed with reflexive thematic analysis. The researcher is a second-language Afrikaans speaker whose own variety is Kaaps, which the paper treats as part of the researcher's positionality.

Results

Adaptation

  • All errors in the forward translations were linguistic: six in the researcher's version and five in the machine translation.
  • The synthesis kept five items' wording from the forward versions and wrote five anew.
  • Pretesters were unfamiliar with stelsel (two), omslagtig (three) and vermoë (one), and two found the wording overly formal.
  • Eight of ten items changed after pretesting. Items 2 and 3 stayed the same, and "cumbersome" in item 8 became moeilik.

Among the six participants who completed both conditions, four had a higher Afrikaans mean and two a higher English mean. Different systems were rated in each condition, so these figures do not show equivalence.

Interviews

  • All three interviewees found the Afrikaans version harder to understand than English, although individual terms rather than meaning caused the difficulty. Two flagged inkonsekwenthede.
  • They saw both versions as asking the same thing and attributed differing answers to different systems.
  • Two would prefer English in a usability study; one would choose Afrikaans only to engage with the language.

Discussion and implications

Understanding a statement and finding it easy to read were different things. Future versions should favour widely understood vocabulary, and offering respondents a choice of language may suit multilingual settings. English difficulties such as "cumbersome" show the problem is not limited to Afrikaans.

Limitations and conclusion

Seven quantitative participants and three interviewees; convenience sampling; different systems per language; English-language interviews, one done by text; self-reported proficiency. The study is not a validation.

The paper's principal contribution is a documented adaptation process and preliminary evidence of how Afrikaans-speaking users interpret AF-SUS. It recommends larger studies with the same systems in both languages and counterbalanced order.
