Honours Research Paper · Afrikaans Arm

Cross-cultural adaptation of the System Usability Scale into Afrikaans

Yazeed Adams · Supervisor: Melissa Densmore · Download Paper (PDF) ↓

This paper developed AF-SUS, a formal Afrikaans adaptation of the System Usability Scale, and examined how bilingual Afrikaans-speaking participants experienced both versions across everyday interactive systems. It constitutes the Afrikaans arm of the SUSEVAL project.

Research questions

  • RQ1: How can the System Usability Scale be cross-culturally adapted into Afrikaans through documented translation frameworks?
  • RQ2: How do Afrikaans-speaking bilingual participants qualitatively experience the English and Afrikaans versions?
  • RQ3: What exploratory differences in quantitative SUS scores emerge between the two versions?

Background

Psychometric instruments cannot be directly transferred into new cultural environments through literal word-for-word translation. This study adopted Ortiz-Gutiérrez and Cruz-Avelar's simplified adaptation of the Beaton et al. guidelines and applied Pan and Fond's coding scheme to systematically classify forward-translation discrepancies. Because Afrikaans encompasses diverse regional varieties and registers, the adaptation deliberately pursued neutral, widely understood phrasing suitable across bilingual demographics.

Methodology

Four-Stage AF-SUS Cross-Cultural Adaptation Protocol
Phase 1 Dual Forward Translation

Two initial translations: one by researcher, one via Google Translate; Pan & Fond error coding.

Phase 2 Expert Synthesis

An Afrikaans linguistic expert reconciled errors, retained 5 item formulations, and re-drafted 5.

Phase 3 Pretesting & Back-Translation

Cognitive debriefing with 6 bilingual participants; 2 independent back-translations to verify intent.

Phase 4 Final Expert Revisions

8 of 10 items revised; "cumbersome" (Item 8) localized to accessible moeilik.

The main study employed a within-subjects experimental design. Seven bilingual participants each evaluated three systems in English and three different systems in Afrikaans; 39 of 42 expected evaluations were successfully completed. Three participants completed in-depth semi-structured interviews, analysed via reflexive thematic analysis. The researcher is a second-language Afrikaans speaker whose primary variety is Kaaps, which the paper transparently acknowledges as part of the researcher's methodological positionality.

Results

Adaptation outcomes

  • Linguistic Error Classification: All errors identified across initial forward translations were linguistic: six in the researcher's translation and five in the machine translation.
  • Item Synthesis: The expert synthesis retained wording from the initial drafts for five items and rewrote five anew to improve natural fluency.
  • Pretesting Friction: Pretest participants were unfamiliar with formal terms such as stelsel (n = 2), omslagtig (n = 3), and vermoë (n = 1), with two noting the phrasing felt excessively rigid or academic.
  • Final Modifications: Eight of the ten questionnaire items were refined following pretesting. Items 2 and 3 remained unchanged, while the problematic English word "cumbersome" in Item 8 was rendered as moeilik.

Descriptive quantitative scores

Descriptive SUS scores by questionnaire language
Language Condition Completed Evaluations Mean SUS Score Standard Deviation (SD)
English (Written) 21 73.57 16.37
Afrikaans (AF-SUS) 18 76.25 14.17

Among the six participants who completed both experimental conditions, four exhibited a higher mean score in the Afrikaans condition and two in the English condition. Because different systems were rated in each condition, these exploratory figures do not indicate measurement equivalence.

Participant interview themes

“I understood the Afrikaans sentences, but I had to read them twice. English tech terms are just second nature to me now.”

  • Comprehension vs. Cognitive Ease: All three interviewees found the Afrikaans questionnaire harder to read quickly than English, although individual formal terms rather than overall conceptual meaning caused the friction. Two participants specifically flagged inkonsekwenthede as challenging.
  • Perceived Equivalence: Participants viewed both language versions as asking identical underlying questions, attributing variations in answers to genuine differences between the evaluated software systems.
  • Evaluation Preference: Two of the three interviewees indicated they would prefer English in future usability testing; one participant expressed a desire for Afrikaans solely to support and engage with the language.

Discussion and implications

A crucial distinction emerged between comprehending a statement and finding it effortless to read. Future questionnaire adaptations should prioritize widely understood vernacular vocabulary over hyper-formal terminology. In multilingual environments like South Africa, offering respondents a dynamic choice of language may best accommodate diverse registers and proficiencies. Furthermore, well-documented English stumbling blocks like "cumbersome" confirm that questionnaire friction is not unique to non-English versions.

Limitations and conclusion

Methodological Limitations: The quantitative sample comprised seven participants and three qualitative interviewees recruited via convenience sampling; different systems were evaluated per language; interviews were conducted in English (one asynchronously by text); and linguistic proficiency was self-reported. This study represents an exploratory adaptation, not a formal psychometric validation.

The principal contribution of this research is a transparently documented adaptation methodology, preliminary evidence of how bilingual South African users interpret AF-SUS, and publication of the finalized AF-SUS item wording for future empirical researchers.
